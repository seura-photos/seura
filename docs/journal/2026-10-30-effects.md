# Effects: black and white, sepia, and the vignette (M4 slice 6)

- **Built while Nevada was away,** after they merged crop mode (#173) and said to go ahead with slice 6. Claude Code chose the formulas and the panel's behaviour below. The formulas went to the edit format as the appendix's next pull request, for the design session to review. The choices it made for the panel are listed in the pull request, for Nevada and the design session.
- **Black and white** blends each channel toward the pixel's luminance. At 100 it's the same as saturation at −100.
- **Sepia** blends toward the luminance in a warm brown tone, scaled to luminance 1 so the photo keeps its brightness. The tone, (1.25, 0.97, 0.56) before scaling, is about the hue of the familiar CSS `sepia()` filter, worked back to linear light. White comes out with red above 1, which clips to a cream.
- **The vignette** is an ellipse fitted to the cropped frame, so it follows the photo's shape and is the same at any resolution. r is 0 at the center, 1 at the middle of each edge, and √2 at the corners. The vignette starts at r = 1 − size/100 and grows smoothly (a smoothstep) to its full amount at the corners.
- **The first vignette was wrong for lightening.** It scaled the color by up to 3 stops each way. Claude Code drew the formulas on a generated scene (sky, sun, grass, a gray ramp) to judge them. A positive amount clipped the sky's channels unevenly and turned it cyan. Now darkening scales toward black and lightening blends toward white, so hues hold, and ±100 reaches black or white at the very corners.
- **The shader** places the vignette by the pixel's place in the result, not in the photo, so tiles of an export and zoomed-in parts of the preview agree. A test renders a 2300 × 1700 photo in tiles with a cropped vignette against the definition.
- **On and off in the panel** (INT-4): Off is the neutral setting, which removes the operation, as the format advises. Turning an effect on again brings it back as it was while the Editor is open, or at its default: full black and white or sepia, and a vignette of −40 at size 50. Double-clicking an effect's slider sets that default rather than 0, since 0 would turn it off. A slider dragged to 0 stays under the pointer, with its button showing Off, until the shown edits are replaced (another photo, undo, redo, revert). That way a slider never vanishes mid-drag, and keyboard focus isn't lost when a key reaches 0.
- **The render check passed** on Windows (largest difference 1 level on WebGPU, 0 on WebGL2) and on Nevada's MacBook (0 on both), run by the Mac session.
- **On the MacBook, PRF-6** ran every slider at 60 frames a second, WebKit's cap, at every size on both paths. One frame in the whole run took over 33 ms (Saturation, WebGL2, 12 MP, 38 ms); the effects' longest was 20 ms. Its settings now include partial black and white and sepia and a vignette, so the colors before them still count.
- **PRF-6's measurement** now turns the effects on before dragging, so their sliders are measured and every drag draws them. Strength and size sliders are dragged within their own 0 to 100, not past 0. On Windows, every slider on 12, 24 and 48 megapixel photos ran at 114 frames a second or more, on WebGPU and on WebGL2. The effects' sliders ran at 120.
- **The first measurement missed the effects.** The Editor announces a photo as drawn just before React re-renders, so the effect buttons were still disabled when the script clicked them. Only one click in six landed. The script now waits for the buttons to be enabled.
- **The second run found a real gap (#174).** On the last photo, the script turned the effects off and the app quit within the second. The "off" was never saved, so the photo kept its vignette in the measurement's own library. Nothing saves a waiting change when the app quits, so a user who moves a slider and quits at once loses that change. The measurement now waits for its saves; the app's quit is left for #174.
- **Nevada found Revert hard to reach.** With Effects added, the panel is taller than the default window and scrolls. Revert to original is last, so the user has to scroll to reach it. Its confirmation (Cancel in place, the question and Revert below) then lands below the window's edge. Someone who doesn't know to scroll just sees their button turn into Cancel. Nevada noted that scrolling the panel to show it would put the pointer over the confirm button, which INT-5 rules out, and asked for suggestions. Claude Code suggested pinning Revert in a footer at the panel's bottom, with the confirmation opening upward, and wrote it up for the design session with the other options (the top of the panel, the ⋯ menu, opening upward alone).
